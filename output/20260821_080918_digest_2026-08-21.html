General Contractor Found Not Liable for Subcontractor Employee's Injury

Source: www.insurancejournal.com · Score: 0.85
Why it matters: Defines 'retained control' standard for general contractors, impacting construction liability and insurance risk.
  • Mass. Supreme Judicial Court affirmed summary judgment for general contractor Skyway—no liability for injuries to a subcontractor’s employee because the subcontract left means/methods and safety to the subcontractor and Skyway neither retained nor exercised control over safety measures (a building permit listing Skyway as general contractor was insufficient).
  • Court applied the retained-control doctrine (Corsetti): a general contractor is liable only if it retained the right to control aspects of the work, including initiating/maintaining safety programs; absent express contractual safety obligations or concrete safety actions, duty of care can be lacking as a matter of law.
  • Negligent-hiring claim by the injured subcontractor employee failed—evidence of prior OSHA citations against the subcontractor did not create general-contractor liability where the GC had not retained safety control; summary judgment was affirmed on both negligence and negligent-hiring claims.
  • Practical implications for insurers: ensure contracts explicitly allocate safety responsibilities and control language (retained-control clauses increase exposure); consider endorsements/coverage terms and additional-insured wording, and preserve summary-judgment/choice-of-law defenses when underwriting and handling claims.

OpEd: Offshore wind – A hundred identical things

Source: www.globalreinsurance.com · Score: 0.82
Why it matters: Covers offshore wind insurance challenges, policy wording, serial loss exposure, and NOWIC—a market-relevant topic for Lloyd's.
  • Subsea/export cables drive the majority and the highest-severity offshore-wind claims (though they are a small fraction of project cost) because faults cause long outages — so losses are principally consequential business interruption, not just repair costs.
  • Offshore-wind projects are collections of many identical, repetitive assets; they are usually insured on WELCAR (an oil-and-gas form) that assumes a few bespoke units, creating a mismatch in accumulation profile and underwriting assumptions.
  • The defective-part wording is ambiguous for wind farms: whether a failed protection system, a cable length, or the array is the ‘part’ changes indemnity dramatically. Serial failures therefore create occurrence/aggregation disputes that determine the number of deductibles and limits engaged; resolution should be driven by engineering and factual evidence, not schedule drafting.
  • The market is responding with NOWIC — a purpose-built wind wording that defaults to cover for design/material/workmanship with a dedicated serial-defect limitation. Reinsurers should focus on clarifying unit-of-loss, modelling accumulation, and insisting on evidence-based contractual definitions and aggregation mechanics.
