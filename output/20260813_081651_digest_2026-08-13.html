Viewpoint: Is it Time to Rethink the 'Secondary-Peril' Label and Reclassify Risk?

Source: www.insurancejournal.com · Score: 0.78
Why it matters: Covers multi-peril risk, modeling, pricing, and treaty design relevant to the Lloyd's market.
  • Secondary perils (convective storms, wildfires, floods, hail) have escalated from ‘‘tail’’ contributors to material drivers of industry loss—portfolios are increasingly multi-hazard and the ‘‘secondary’’ label is becoming inadequate.
  • Three structural drivers: climate variability increasing frequency/severity, growing and more exposed built assets in hazard zones, and compounded/interacting perils that generate joint-tail losses.
  • Catastrophe modelling is shifting to integrated multi‑peril frameworks: explicitly modelling peril dependencies and tail correlation, using climate‑informed scenarios, dynamic exposure/vulnerability, ensemble model outputs, and stronger model governance and validation.
  • Pricing, capital and treaty architecture must reflect aggregated multi‑peril risk—expect premium realism, reduced diversification benefits, peril‑agnostic layers, multi‑peril triggers, parametric solutions, cross‑peril retrocession/sidecars, and dynamic attachment mechanics tied to concentration and seasonality.

AI's Volatile Power Demand Is Damaging Its Own Data Centers

Source: www.insurancejournal.com · Score: 0.75
Why it matters: Discusses reliability risks, downtime costs, and insurance implications for AI data centers, relevant to Lloyd's risk market.
  • Millisecond-scale power swings at AI data centers are causing accelerated wear and premature failures in generators, turbines, batteries and other electrical equipment (examples: cracked turbines, broken engine cranks, batteries needing replacement in weeks/months), increasing frequency and severity of physical-damage claims.
  • Secondary hazards—such as electrical arc flashes—can damage extremely expensive GPUs/servers, while even minutes of downtime produce large revenue losses; this raises insurers’ exposure to significant business-interruption and contingent BI claims tied to compute outages.
  • Many facilities were not designed for these atypical dynamic loads and often lack adequate power-conditioning or mitigation (batteries, capacitors, flywheels, microgrids); this suggests faster-than-expected depreciation, higher replacement costs, potential latent defects and gaps in standard policy assumptions.
  • Underwriting implications: reassess pricing and limits, incorporate scenario modelling for rapid-load failures and BI exposures, condition coverage on mitigations/maintenance and monitoring, and engage with clients on engineering standards to manage aggregation/systemic grid risk.
