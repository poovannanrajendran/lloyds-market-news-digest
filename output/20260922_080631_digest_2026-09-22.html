Judge Throws Out Bulk of Economic Claims Over Baltimore Bridge Tragedy

Source: www.insurancejournal.com · Score: 0.82
Why it matters: Maritime insurance claims, liability limits, and settlements from a major bridge collapse.
  • Judge dismissed the bulk of economic-loss claims against the Dali’s owner/operator, applying the 1927 Robins Dry Dock rule that bars recovery for pure economic loss absent a proprietary interest in damaged property—many business‑interruption and other indirect claims by private firms and insurers were thus thrown out.
  • Remaining viable claims are narrow: Baltimore’s claim for alleged damage to a 72‑inch submerged water main, Baltimore County’s attempt to prove proprietary interests in waterways/shorelines, and four cargo‑damage claims may proceed.
  • Owners invoked the Limitation of Liability Act (seeking to cap exposure at the vessel’s value, ~$44M) but have nevertheless settled many claims well above that limit (e.g., federal cleanup $102M, state $2.5M, ACE/Chubb paid $350M), creating complex allocation and subrogation issues.
  • Implications for insurers: decreased prospect of broad indirect/BI recoveries from the shipowner/operator; greater emphasis on property‑specific and cargo claims, coordination of subrogation efforts, and careful handling of coverage/allocation given disparate settlements and limitation proceedings.

‘And/or’ wording sinks bid to escape listeria outbreak coverage - Business Insurance

Source: www.businessinsurance.com · Score: 0.72
Why it matters: Coverage dispute and ambiguous policy language impacting underwriters; Lloyd's market relevance.
  • Background: In Scottsdale Ins. Co. v. Bruce Packing Co. (D. Ore.), Scottsdale sought a declaration that its 2024 excess policy’s “Communicable Disease Exclusion” absolved it of duty to defend/indemnify Bruce Packing after a 2024 listeria contamination and recall of ~12 million pounds of product triggered multiple suits.
  • Key legal finding: The court held listeria is an infectious (but not contagious) disease and that the exclusion’s definition—using the phrase “infectious and/or contagious”—is ambiguous because Oregon courts treat “and/or” as inherently unclear.
  • Coverage outcome: Because the ambiguity could be read both to include and to exclude the loss, Oregon law’s rule construing ambiguities against the drafter (the insurer) required denial of Scottsdale’s motion to avoid coverage (insurer remains obligated to defend/possibly indemnify).
  • Practical implications for insurers: Draft and underwrite communicable-disease exclusions with precise, unambiguous language; expect courts to apply contra proferentem to ambiguous terms; review underwriting, policy wordings and claims handling for foodborne vs. person-to-person disease distinctions.
