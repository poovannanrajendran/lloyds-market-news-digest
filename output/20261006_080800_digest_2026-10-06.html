QBE must show harm to deny discrimination claim: Appeals court - Business Insurance

Source: www.businessinsurance.com · Score: 0.65
Why it matters: Covers an insurance coverage dispute and how late notice must show harm, with implications for insurers and EPLI market practices.
  • 10th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals reversed summary judgment for QBE in Geneva Rock Products v. QBE, holding under Utah law an insurer must prove it was prejudiced before denying coverage for untimely notice and remanding the case for a factual determination of harm.
  • The dispute involved a claims-made employment practices policy with a 60-day post-expiration reporting provision; Utah’s statutory exemption for policies requiring notice before expiration did not apply because the policy included that 60-day grace period.
  • Key legal takeaway for insurers: when a policy allows post-policy reporting, carriers generally cannot automatically deny coverage for late notice — the insurer bears the evidentiary burden to demonstrate that the delay caused actual prejudice.
  • Practical actions for insurers and brokers: review policy wording and state notice statutes, promptly document any prejudice (investigation/defense/reserve impacts), reassess notice-driven declinations and reserving/defense practices; the ruling shifts the burden but does not require payment absent a finding of harm.
