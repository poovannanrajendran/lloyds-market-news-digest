Property reinsurance investing offers more manageable tail risk than perceived: Agecroft - Artemis.bm

Source: www.artemis.bm · Score: 0.80
Why it matters: Covers property catastrophe reinsurance, pricing cycles, capacity, and renewal dynamics—core topics for the reinsurance market in which Lloyd's operates.
  • Tail risk in property catastrophe reinsurance is explicitly modeled and priced (full exceedance probability curves: no‑loss, median, 1‑in‑10/20/100), so downside is more transparent than in equities/credit where tails are largely undisclosed.
  • Disciplined underwriting and carefully constructed portfolios can materially manage downside volatility; the asset class contains a built‑in recovery mechanism—large loss years reduce capacity, tighten pricing and improve subsequent returns.
  • Historical cycles show pricing driven by supply/demand (e.g., post‑Katrina repricing, 2016–22 outflows then recent rebound); Agecroft’s forward modeled returns for well‑structured property cat portfolios are mid/high single digits to low double digits, competitive with fixed‑income alternatives.
  • Property reinsurance exhibits low correlation to traditional assets because returns are driven by physical events rather than market sentiment, offering meaningful portfolio diversification.

How to tackle insurance policy application fraud – Smartnumbers

Source: www.insurancetimes.co.uk · Score: 0.72
Why it matters: Discusses insurance fraud trends, cross-sector risk, and data-sharing controls relevant to Lloyd's market.
  • Application fraud is rising and increasingly sophisticated — ABI recorded 684,800 prevented application-fraud cases in 2023–24 (a 7.4% year‑on‑year increase), indicating more than opportunistic misrepresentation.
  • Organised crime is driving growth: ghost broking and use of stolen or AI-generated (synthetic) identities are rising, with low‑KYC lines (eg travel) used to build credible financial histories for wider fraud.
  • Contact centres are being probed after digital blocks — fraudsters move between channels; phone numbers (even when spoofed) are a critical thread to link suspicious activity across calls, applications and identities.
  • Insurers should pursue collective responses: share contact‑centre intelligence and phone‑number denylists across organisations and sectors to surface cross‑company fraud patterns and disrupt organised operations.
