Insurers Want a Bigger Slice of the Bank Risk Transfer Boom

Source: www.insurancejournal.com · Score: 0.82
Why it matters: Covers significant risk transfers (SRTs), insurers' activity, and bank-insurer hedging, aligned with Lloyd's market news.
  • Strong near‑term growth opportunity — surveyed insurers expect to participate in ~50% more SRTs than in 2025; insurers protected €4.7bn of SRT tranches in 2025 (vs €2.7bn in 2024) and have €10.9bn of outstanding protections on portfolios tied to €366bn of loans.
  • Product mix and insurer role — banks use both funded (cash collateral) and unfunded SRTs; insurers predominantly provide unfunded credit guarantees (96 unfunded protections in 2025 vs 78 in 2024), with demand strongest in corporate loans and asset‑based finance, then residential mortgages.
  • Risk and regulatory implications — SRTs are used to boost banks’ solvency ratios and free up capital, but regulators (eg, Bank of England) warn that reliance on insurers to honor unfunded guarantees can increase bank exposure in stress; insurers must assess capital, liquidity, counterparty and concentration risk when writing these protections.
  • Geography and key market players — EU portfolios account for 63% of unfunded references (US 17%, other Europe incl. UK 12%). Active banks include BNP Paribas, MUFG, Erste, Santander and UniCredit; participating insurers include Munich Re, AXA, AXIS and Liberty Mutual; transactions often placed via arrangers/brokers (Howden, Marsh & McLennan).

A drier risk landscape – is UK insurance keeping pace with drought?

Source: www.insurancetimes.co.uk · Score: 0.78
Why it matters: Drought risk and its insurance market implications (subsidence, wildfire, flooding) are directly relevant to Lloyd's market risk and coverage trends.
  • Drought is becoming a material UK risk driver—raising subsidence, wildfire and infrastructure stresses—and its losses can surface weeks to months after dry periods, with hardened ground also increasing surface-water flood risk when heavy rain follows.
  • Claims data show tangible impact: notable subsidence spikes after 2022 and 2025 heatwaves, average domestic subsidence claim ~£20,000 and £72m paid in Q2 2026; losses often manifest 1–2 months post-heat with longer tail costs.
  • Drought should be treated as an interconnected risk rather than a standalone peril—subsidence, wildfire and flooding are correlated and create aggregation exposure across multiple lines of business and geographies.
  • Industry awareness is outpacing behavioural change: demand for new protections (e.g., wildfire cover) is rising but has not yet translated into widespread policy changes; insurers should revisit underwriting, aggregation modelling, pricing, and customer risk-engagement strategies with emphasis on secondary and delayed losses.
